Three levels of disclosure

102 models across eight providers is not a table anyone reads. The page opens in stages, and each level is chosen to answer one question:

  • Closed provider panel — which provider is worth opening? So it carries a price range, not just a count. The range is computed over the standard tier only, and it is the reason a closed panel is worth its row.
  • Open panel — what do these models cost? Standard and cache, in both directions, one row per model.
  • Open model row — what else could it cost? Description, specifications, and every remaining tier.

modelsOpen and modelRowOpen are Sets on LogApp.state, the same contract as navOpen and treeOpen: a poll tick or a filter change never collapses what the reader opened.

The columns follow each provider's own vocabulary

Providers disagree about what a cache is called — cache_read, cached_input, context_cache — and only Anthropic prices cache writes. Because the table lives inside a provider panel, columnsFor() derives its columns from the tier names that provider actually uses, instead of a normalised label matching nobody:

Everything displaced by that rule is in the tier panel, and the +N chip counts exactly how many distinct tier names are waiting there.

Four rules about numbers

  • An em dash is never a zero. A dash means the provider does not sell that tier. Four models in the registry are genuinely free and show $0; that zero has to keep meaning zero.
  • A model with no standard tier says what it is priced by. 19 of them — the realtime family is per-modality, the retired deep-research models were batch-only. Their headline row is all dashes, so a generated note follows it rather than leaving the reader to open the row and guess.
  • A launch price is shown as the price you pay. An intro_…_until_ tier still in force displaces the standard rate in the headline, marked intro, with the end date and the following price in its tooltip. The tier panel tags it in force or expired.
  • Ratios are per direction. gpt-5.4's long-context tier is +100% on input and +50% on output; one shared "vs standard" column would have to pick one and be wrong about the other.

The tier panel joins two lists on tier name

The registry stores input_token[] and output_token[] independently, but their names overlap heavily. tierRows() unions them — input tiers in tier_order, then output-only tiers — so rows are tier names and columns are direction. Nine scattered numbers become six comparable lines, and the asymmetry becomes visible rather than being something you have to notice: no provider in the registry prices cached output, because caching is an input-side concept.

This page must not use cost-model.js. That module resolves a counter to the one rate that applies, walking a fallback chain until something matches — correct for pricing a request, wrong for a catalogue, where a cache column that quietly fell back to the standard rate would tell the reader the provider caches for free. model-catalog.js reads tiers exactly as stored, and formats them with its own fmtPrice: CostModel.fmtCost rounds below a cent to <$0.01, which would destroy $0.0375 — a real published rate, not a rounding error.
